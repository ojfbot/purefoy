“Empire of Light” Searchlight Pictures

 

EMPIRE OF LIGHT
Looking at Lighting

‘Empire of Light’ was filmed in and around Margate, Kent. We were first attracted to the abandoned cinema complex called Dreamland, which was perfectly set on the town’s promenade and offered various interior options along with a good rooftop.

Dreamland Location

 

THE LOBBY SET

But, the lobby as it existed in Dreamland was too confined for the scenes in the script so, as no other viable location was available, the alternative was to build a set. This could have been a set built on stage but, as the exterior view was important for both the action as scripted and the mood of the piece, we turned to the idea of building a set on location. Luckily for us, a building had been demolished a few doors away from Dreamland leaving an open lot that looked out on the same sea view. To have this pivotal set within a short walking distance of a main location was a considerable advantage in dealing with a tight schedule and the constantly shifting weather conditions.

The Lobby Set

 

The lobby was designed by Mark Tildesley and much of the lighting was either built into the set or added in collaboration with our set dresser, Kamlan Man. What was built-in included extensive cove lighting that ran around the ceilings on both floor levels and skirted the edge of two domes that were specifically designed to boost the illumination of the central staircase and the concessions stand.

The Lobby Ceiling

 

The lights we used here were Bi-color LED strips. This gave our lighting console programmer and operator, Galo Dominguez, the ability to adjust both the intensity and color of the interior as the quality of the exterior daylight shifted, or to adjust individual strips of LEDs relative to the camera angle. Other practical lights, either built by the art department or brought in by the set dressers, were rigged with full color LED Astera tubes or bulbs, so that these too could be adjusted in the same way. The central chandelier, which was an important visual feature as well as a key lighting element, proved to be a challenge. Not only did it needed to be completely rewired but I also asked Kamlan to find or manufacture some glass diffusers to be less opaque than those that came with the fixture and large enough to contain the slightly taller than standard Astera bulbs.

Another design element within the lobby was a false skylight centered above the main staircase. To light this, as if it were open to the daylight, we used Gemini LED units. As with all our other LEDs we were able to balance these to the color temperature of the natural daylight that lit the lobby through glass doors that faced the promenade.

The Empire Lobby Set Daytime Lighting Rig

 

Lobby Exterior “Control” Rig

 

To rehearse and shoot some of the night work inside the Lobby – at least those angles facing away from the doors – a pipe rig was built into the construction of the set exterior to which we could easily attach 20’ x 20’ frames carrying solids. This pipe also helped when we needed to control the light balance between the exterior and interior during the day, whether by hanging a curtain of diffusion for lamps to project through or by adding nets to restrict the existing daylight when that proved too harsh.

For the most part, I shot any wide views of the lobby by adjusting what was built into the set but for closer work I made extensive use of the 5 x Q5F Fiilex LED lamps, which we carried for this purpose in our ‘floor package’. During prep in London, John Higgins, my gaffer, and I had made some extensive studies of the latest available LED lighting units and it seemed to us that the Fiilex, with or without its soft box and honeycomb attachments, gave us all the options we would need. Whereas with traditional Fresnel lamps I would have had to add scrims and gels to adjust the intensity and color of individual lamps, now Galo could do this on his iPad. By using the soft box attachments, we could easily gang up a series of these lamps to create one soft source whilst also making it relatively easy to prevent their reflection appearing in the glass of a door or picture frame.

SCREEN ONE

The Dreamland cinema complex provided us with an exterior, the dilapidated upstairs ballroom and a rooftop that opened out towards the beach, but the theaters themselves were more problematic. However, so as not to move from our base in Margate, Mark Tildesley came up with a plan to turn the main theater from the full-on Bingo Hall, which was presented to us on scouting, to a space that reflected what Sam had in mind when he wrote the script.

The Bingo Hall

 

The lighting Rig

 

As the main theater had been broken up, and what had once been a balcony extended above the auditorium as a separate screening room, space for lighting was limited to the one high area of original ceiling left. Given this lack of space and to allow for access to our lighting rig if there were any adjustments needed, John and I decided that our only option was to suspend a rig from above the ceiling using chain hoists fed through the ceiling. A major problem was getting access to this crawl space, and I won’t go into the complexities of dealing with asbestos, structural engineering, and safety issues here. Suffice to say John Higgins is relentless.

For some scenes I wanted a cool overhead light, which appeared as if it were from fluorescent sources. For other scenes I was looking for a warm soft wash of light and, for the premiere, something more centered on the stage. To create the soft wash, I went for a circular array of household style bulbs, which is a technique I have often used in the past. A series of aluminum ring lights would be suitably lightweight, simple to construct and, now with the benefit of Astera LED bulbs, Galo could pre-program some settings to reflect each change of scene.

Screen One – section – lighting plan

 

To light the center of the stage we tied into structural supports above the ceiling to hang a separate pipe rigged with a dozen 300-watt Fresnel lamps, each with individual dimmer control. For the ‘Chariots of Fire’ premiere these lamps were dimmed in sequence so that the center of the row was quite hot but the outside lamps very warm and dim. This gave a soft spotlight effect but with a warm edge to it. The premiere was the only time I would use these lamps so their focus and the light levels they gave me could be pre-set. Consequently, I was not concerned that they were not LEDs and, also important, these more traditional lamps were far cheaper.

Lighting in progress

 

The above picture shows the effect of these Fresnel lamps as well as the overhead ring lights. To each end of the truss that supported the ring lights we added two 650-watt Fresnel lamps. The outermost of these two lamps was snooted and focused on the statues above the side exit doors while the second lamp was set as a wider wash on the doorframe itself. In the same image you can see on-stage a truss which was to become the frame of the projection screen and to the sides of which will be a soffit light of the same kind that details the side walls of the theater. On the wall to the rear of the theater, and primarily in use as a light source during the premiere, we mounted some fixtures designed for the purpose by the art department.

Mark built the projection booth, which would have originally been above the balcony, into the main auditorium as an exterior shell only and inside we installed two digital projectors. The interior of the booth, with its carbon arc projectors and viewing ports, was built as a separate set in a warehouse nearby.

THE PROJECTION BOOTH

A Test Image

 

The above image was shot during prep at a facility with the same carbon arc projectors as we were going to bring to our set. We shot this as a camera test for the Alexa LF but also as one of a series of angles that we might want to replicate on set. What was instantly obvious was that the projectors themselves would serve as a major light source and that we needed a screen to project onto if we were going to embrace the kind of reflections that naturally occur in such an environment. Although the space outside our set’s projector windows was nothing equivalent to that within the real theater Rod McLean, who had come in for a few weeks as an additional art director, arranged for a false perspective screen to stand in for something many times further away.

A general exterior lighting plan

 

The above plan was made so that our rigging crew and the location department would know exactly what I needed in advance of shooting and how much work in total would be required around the Dreamland location. The only daytime lighting that I wanted rigged in advance was so as to project light into the small private room in which Hilary and Michael care for the pigeon and from the alley through the windows of the passage leading to the upstairs ballroom. For each of these rigs we used 2’ x 1’ Gemini LED panels in arrays of five or 6 lamps with a light grid cloth as a front diffusion.

The night work required a variety of individual sources. For the rooftop the art department constructed a circular skylight, that matched in design one that existed to the rear of the building, as well as a canopy with a neon motif for over the access door. Both these elements were crucial in lighting the scene that takes place between Hilary and Stephen on the rooftop. Sam had talked through how he imagined the conversation that takes place early in the scene would be staged and whatever I used to source this part of the scene I did not want it to wash out the rest of the rooftop. Specifically, I was aiming to silhouette the actors when they were at the parapet and looking at the fireworks, so the purpose built ‘skylight’ solved the problem. To create the effect of light coming from the interior below we mounted MR16 style LED bulbs on a 3’ diameter aluminum ring fixed to the ceiling of the set piece. This circle of light bounced off a white sheet that we laid on the roof on which our fake skylight sat and with a little frosting on the glass appeared as one soft source.

Some things changed from my original drawing. For instance, the spots onto the bare wall to the left of the location were never rigged while additional lights, 2K open face Blondes, were used to wash up the front of a few distant buildings. On the day of the shoot, we brought around two Scissor lifts to facilitate lighting Hilary and Stephen when they stand at the parapet to watch the firework display. Here, I used a 20’ line of Double Rainbow LEDs to match and softly augment the light coming from the neon signage below.

A festoon along the promenade was an ever-present element of any seaside town in the 1980s, much as it still is today, but, unluckily for us, what had once existed in Margate had long been torn down. This was probably the most difficult and thorny issue to deal with, both for production and the location department. Needing to be considered was the cost, the structural integrity of the lampposts, the power supply required for this half mile of festoon, security issues as well as the permits required from all the relevant authorities. Again, thanks to the perseverance of Emma Pill, our location manager, as well as John Higgins and his crew, these lights magically appeared just in time for our first day/night of shooting.

Hilary walks toward her apartment building

 

The section of the sea front that Hillary walks along had no practical lighting. I did not want to create a ‘moonlight’ because it had only recently been snowing in our story line and I thought a pretty blue light would only distract from the lonely and melancholic feeling I was after. For an alternative, I worked with our set dressing department to find suitable lamp fixtures that would be both visually appropriate and easily mounted on the posts that defined the walkway. The lamps they provided matched others that already existed on the sea front in Margate and, more importantly to me, the globe design of each could provide a perfect pool of omnidirectional light. The globes were made of a clear plastic so, for a central light source that was not only cool but also bright, we took simple strips of bi-color LEDs and wrapped them around a short section of 3” plastic pipe. I liked the idea of mixing the cool of a lamp close to camera with others in the background that were warm so using this LED rig made this an easy option.

The streetlamp that lights Hilary’s apartment building in the background of the shot is augmented by 3 x Red Heads. These were mounted close together without using their barn doors. Each was focused to rake across the face of the building and lined up with the head of the streetlamp. These were set to hand dimmers on the street beside the lamppost. For the next shot, in which Hilary approached the doorway and we were now looking down the building and towards the sea, these same lamps were adjusted 90º to remain behind the practical fixture as seen by the camera.

The Bus Stop

 

Here, a streetlamp was removed, and the original bus stop sign was replaced. To create a pool of light around the new bus stop we brought in a period style streetlight that sat on a mobile base and we fixed this to camera when we set up our main wide shot of the action. Picture 12 is another example of the kind of references I made for our location and rigging departments before shooting began.
